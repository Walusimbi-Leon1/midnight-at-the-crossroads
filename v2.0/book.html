Chapter 2 — The Last Bus

00:00.

The digital clock above Platform 7 blinked the change like it was announcing something important, and maybe it was, because my bus—the 11:58 to Saint Mary's—was pulling out of the bay with the kind of smug finality that only a departing night bus can manage. I stood there with my lunch bag in one hand and my scrubs still smelling faintly of antiseptic and regret, watching the red taillights shrink into the dark like a joke I was too tired to get.

"Unbelievable," I said to no one, because that's what you do at midnight in an empty bus station when the universe has personally decided to inconvenience you.

I'd done the math. I always did the math. Shift ends at 23:30. Walk to the station takes twelve minutes if I cut through the hospital parking lot and skip the underpass. The 11:58 was my bus. My bus. The one I'd caught every Friday for six months, the one with the driver who nodded at me like we were old friends, the one that got me home with just enough time to eat something questionable and sleep before my next shift swallowed me whole.

But tonight, Mrs. Okafor in Room 4 had decided to code at 23:15, and you don't tell a woman's heart to wait while you catch a bus. So I'd stayed. I'd done my job. I'd watched her come back to us, gray-faced and furious at being dragged from whatever peaceful nothing she'd been heading toward, and I'd felt that familiar mix of triumph and exhaustion that only nurses understand.

And now I was here. At midnight. In a bus station that smelled like stale coffee and ambition, watching my ride disappear.

"Fantastic," I said, louder this time.

"Bit of a strong word for a bus leaving," said a voice behind me.

I turned.

He was leaning against the timetable board like he'd been there for years, which was possible, given the state of his jacket. It was denim, distressed in that way that could have been fashionable or just tired, and under it he wore a t-shirt that said something about a band I'd never heard of. His hair was a mess—the kind of messy that suggests you've been running your hands through it while thinking too hard about something—and he was holding a guitar case like it was a fifth limb.

He was also, undeniably, the most interesting thing I'd seen all day. Which says more about my day than it does about him, but still.

"Sorry," I said, because I'm British about these things even when I'm not British. "Didn't realize I had an audience."

"Audience implies I was watching," he said, pushing off the timetable board with a shoulder. "I was more... in the vicinity. Observing. From a respectful distance."

"That's watching."

"That's narrating." He grinned, and it was the kind of grin that probably got him out of speeding tickets and into trouble in equal measure. "You missed your bus."

"I'm aware."

"Last one tonight, that." He nodded toward the empty bay. "The 11:58's the last one until the 4:35 milk run. You're stuck here for roughly four and a half hours."

I felt my stomach drop. "You're joking."

"I never joke about timetables." He gestured at the board above him, which I now noticed had a hand-scrawled amendment taped over the schedule. *11:58 — Final departure. No replacement service. Apologies for inconvenience.* "Tragic, really. The romance of the last bus. It's like watching a relationship end in real time."

"You're very chatty for someone I've never met."

"You're very hostile for someone who just missed their ride. We're even." He shifted his guitar case to his other hand and stuck out his right one. "Bayo."

I looked at his hand like it might bite me. "Jo."

"Just Jo?"

"Just Jo." I shook it, briefly, because I'm not a monster. His grip was warm and firm, and he smelled like sandalwood and something vaguely like rain. "And I'm not hostile. I'm exhausted. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. Hostile implies I have the energy to care about you. Exhausted means I wish you were a chair so I could sit down."

He laughed, and it was a good laugh—genuine, uncalculated, the kind that crinkles his eyes in a way I definitely didn't notice. "I like you. You're honest."

"I'm tired. It's not the same thing."

"It's practically the same thing." He glanced at the clock. "So, Jo-just-Jo, what's your plan? You going to sit here until the 4:35 and traumatize the pigeons, or are you going to let me buy you a coffee?"

I blinked. "It's midnight."

"Which is when coffee is most necessary. There's a vending machine by the ticket office. It's terrible, but it's hot, and it's something to do."

I should have said no. I should have found a bench, put my head down, and waited out the four hours in the kind of miserable silence that makes for good stories later. But the thing about exhaustion is that it lowers your defenses, and the thing about midnight is that it makes everything feel slightly unreal, and the thing about this man with his messy hair and his guitar case and his terrible-coffee vending machine proposal was that he seemed like the most interesting alternative to sitting alone with my thoughts that I'd encountered in a very long time.

"Fine," I said. "But if the coffee's terrible, I'm holding you personally responsible."

"Deal." He started walking toward the ticket office, then paused, looking back over his shoulder. "For the record? I think you're more interesting than the last bus anyway."

I followed him, because I didn't have a better idea, and because something about this whole situation felt like the beginning of a story I hadn't asked to be in but was suddenly curious to read.

The vending machine was worse than advertised. It was the kind of machine that looks like it's been through a war, with a cracked front panel and a button for "Cappuccino" that had been taped over with the word "NO" in permanent marker. Bayo fed it two pound coins and it made a sound like a dying robot before producing a paper cup of something brownish that might have been coffee in a previous life.

"See?" he said, handing it to me. "Terrible. Just like I promised."

I took a sip. It was lukewarm, vaguely bitter, and tasted faintly of dish soap. "This is genuinely offensive."

"I know. It's a gift." He'd gotten himself a black tea that looked equally suspect, and we stood there by the machine, two strangers in a fluorescent-lit limbo, drinking our terrible drinks like we'd been doing it for years.

"So," he said, "Jo-just-Jo. What's a nurse doing missing her bus at midnight?"

"I didn't tell you I was a nurse."

"You didn't have to." He gestured at my scrubs. "The uniform's a bit of a giveaway. Also the smell. Antiseptic. Very specific."

"Stalker."

"Observant. There's a difference."

"That's what I said about hostile versus exhausted. You're stealing my lines."

"I'm borrowing them. I'll return them eventually." He took a sip of his tea and made a face. "So. The bus. What happened?"

I hesitated. There was something about the way he asked—genuine, unhurried, like he actually wanted to know—that made me want to tell him the truth. "There was a patient. Mrs. Okafor. Her heart stopped right as I was about to leave."

"And?"

"And I brought her back." I shrugged, trying to pretend it wasn't a big deal. "Took longer than I thought. By the time I got out, the bus was already gone."

"Right." He nodded slowly, like he was processing this. "So you saved someone's life and the reward is missing your last bus and drinking dishwater coffee with a stranger."

"I didn't say I saved her life. I said I brought her back. It's a team effort."

"But you were part of it."

"I was there."

"Jo." He said my name like it meant something. "You were there. That's the part that matters."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I took another sip of my terrible coffee and tried not to think about how his voice had made something in my chest shift slightly. It was probably just the caffeine. It was definitely just the caffeine.

"Your turn," I said, because I needed to change the subject. "What's a musician doing at a bus station at midnight?"

He raised an eyebrow. "Who says I'm a musician?"

"The guitar case. The calluses on your fingers. The way you keep looking at the clock like you're already late for something." I gestured at him with my cup. "Also, the t-shirt. 'The Late Arrivals.' That's a band name if I've ever heard one."

He laughed, surprised. "It's a band. My band. We were supposed to play a gig tonight, but the venue double-booked, so we got bumped. The others went home. I stayed because—" He stopped, and something flickered across his face. "I don't know. I wasn't ready to go home yet."

"Late for everything, are you?"

"Always. It's a curse." He grinned. "Or a talent. Depends on the day."

"Which is it today?"

"Tonight? I think it's a talent." He looked at me, and there was something in his eyes—something warm and slightly reckless—that made me glad I'd said yes to the coffee. "Because if I hadn't been late, I wouldn't have been here to see you miss your bus."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me about a transportation mishap."

"I aim to please."

We stood there for a moment, the vending machine humming its sad little hum, the clock above us ticking toward 00:15. Somewhere in the distance, a train announced its arrival in a voice that sounded bored with the whole affair.

"So," Bayo said, "what do we do for the next four hours?"

I thought about it. I could sit on a bench and stare at the wall. I could scroll through my phone and pretend I wasn't exhausted. I could do what I normally did, which was exist in the gray space between shifts, waiting for time to pass.

Or I could talk to this strange, late, honest man with the guitar and the terrible taste in vending machine beverages.

"I don't know," I said. "What do you usually do when you're stranded at a bus station at midnight?"

"Usually?" He shrugged. "I play. Badly. To an audience of pigeons and the occasional security guard."

"Play for me."

He blinked. "What?"

"Play for me," I repeated. "You've got a guitar. I've got four hours. Show me what you've got."

He looked at me for a long moment, something unreadable passing through his eyes. Then he smiled—a real smile, not the easy grin he'd worn before—and unzipped his guitar case.

"Alright," he said. "But I'm warning you. It's not finished."

"Nothing ever is," I said, and I didn't know why I said it, but it felt true.

He pulled out the guitar—an acoustic, worn and battered, covered in stickers from places I'd never been—and sat down on the nearest bench. He strummed once, twice, testing the tuning, and then he started to play.

It was slow. Warm. A melody that felt like it was trying to remember something important. He didn't sing, just played, his fingers moving over the strings with an ease that made it look like breathing. And I stood there, terrible coffee cooling in my hand, watching him play in the fluorescent light of a bus station that had never felt less like a bus station.

When he finished, he looked up at me, and there was something vulnerable in his expression that made my chest ache.

"So," he said. "What do you think?"

I opened my mouth to say something witty, something dismissive, something that would keep the walls I'd built around myself intact. But what came out instead was:

"I think you should play it again."

And he did.

At 00:45, the security guard—a man named Gerald who I'd seen a hundred times but never spoken to—came over to tell us we had to move. Something about cleaning the platform. We moved to the waiting area, a row of plastic chairs that had been bolted to the floor for reasons no one could remember, and Bayo kept playing.

At 01:20, a woman with a suitcase and a haunted expression asked if we knew when the next bus was. Bayo told her the truth, and she sat down with us, and by 01:45 she was humming along to a song he invented on the spot, something about a woman who left her heart in a bus station and came back to find it waiting for her.

At 02:10, she left to wait on the other platform, and it was just us again.

"Thank you," Bayo said, out of nowhere.

"For what?"

"For staying." He looked at me, and in the dim light of the waiting area, his eyes were impossibly dark and warm. "Most people don't stay. They hear the first song and they're already thinking about how to leave."

"Most people don't hear the first song," I said. "They're too busy checking their phones."

"And you're not?"

"I checked mine." I pulled it out to show him. "One message from my mum asking if I'm safe. One from a colleague asking if I can cover her shift Tuesday. That's it. Nobody else is waiting for me tonight."

"That's either really sad or really peaceful."

"Both," I said. "It's both."

He nodded, like he understood, and I had the strangest feeling that he did.

At 03:15, we'd exhausted the vending machine's entire inventory of terrible beverages and moved on to telling stories. I told him about Mrs. Okafor, who'd been my patient for three months and who called me "that skinny girl with the sharp tongue." He told me about his band—The Late Arrivals, named after his chronic condition—and the gig they'd lost tonight, and the one they'd almost played in Glasgow, and the one that had fallen through in Manchester, and the one he was supposed to play next week in a basement that held thirty people and smelled like damp.

"I'll come," I said, before I could stop myself.

"You don't even know where it is."

"Then you'll have to tell me."

He smiled, slow and real. "I'll tell you."

At 04:10, the lights in the waiting area flickered, and the first signs of dawn started to creep through the grimy windows. The 4:35 bus was announced, and I felt something sink in my chest.

"Your ride," Bayo said.

"My ride," I agreed.

We stood up together, and for a moment we just looked at each other, two strangers who had somehow become something else in the space of four hours and a handful of terrible coffees.

"Will you be here tomorrow?" I asked.

"Tomorrow's a different day."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the best I can do." He picked up his guitar case, slung it over his shoulder. "But if you're here—and if I'm here—"

"You're chronically late. You'll probably miss me."

"I'll probably miss you," he agreed, and there was something in the way he said it that made me want to stay.

But the bus was waiting, and I had a shift in eight hours, and I couldn't be the kind of person who missed another bus for a man she'd just met.

So I said, "Goodbye, Bayo."

And he said, "See you, Jo."

And I got on the bus, and I didn't look back, because if I looked back I might have gotten off again.

The bus pulled away, and I watched the bus station shrink in the window, and I told myself I'd never see him again.

I was wrong.

Chapter 3 — The 4:35

The thing about being a night-shift nurse is that you develop an intimate relationship with the concept of *not sleeping*. You learn the exact hour when your body gives up on the idea of rest and settles instead for a kind of low-grade buzzing existence, powered by caffeine and spite. The bus ride home was forty-seven minutes of that buzzing, my forehead pressed against the cold window, the city sliding past in smears of grey and sodium orange.

I should have been thinking about my bed. I should have been thinking about the shift ahead, about Mrs. Okafor's medications, about the new admit who'd come in at 2 a.m. with a chest infection and a fear of needles. Instead, I was thinking about a man with a battered guitar and a terrible taste in vending machine beverages, and the way his fingers had moved over the strings like they were having a conversation I wasn't quite fluent in yet.

I was thinking about the fact that he'd said *"See you, Jo"* like it was a promise, not a farewell.

I fell asleep somewhere around the ring road and woke up at my stop with a crick in my neck and the faint impression of a melody I couldn't quite remember.

The next night, I told myself I wasn't going to the bus station.

I told myself this at 10 p.m., while I was eating a sad microwave dinner and staring at the wall. I told myself again at 11:15, while I was brushing my teeth and avoiding my own reflection. I told myself at 11:40, while I was putting on my jacket and grabbing my keys.

At 11:52, I was standing on the platform, watching the digital clock tick toward midnight, and I was telling myself that I was here for the coffee.

The vending machine was still humming its sad little hum. The security guard—Gerald, I knew his name now, a small piece of trivia that felt like a secret—was doing his rounds, his torch beam sweeping across the empty platforms. The air smelled like diesel and rain and the particular loneliness of a place where people only ever pass through.

I bought a coffee. I sat on a bench. I waited.

At 00:00, the clock ticked over, and the bus I was supposed to be on—the one I'd missed last night—pulled in, disgorged a handful of sleepy passengers, and pulled out again.

No Bayo.

I told myself I wasn't disappointed. I told myself I was just enjoying the quiet. I told myself a lot of things, and I believed none of them.

At 00:12, the doors to the waiting area swung open, and a man walked in carrying a guitar case.

He looked different in the harsh fluorescent light. Less like a character in a story, more like a person—a real person, with tired eyes and a bruised knuckle on his left hand and hair that looked like he'd been running his fingers through it for hours. He was wearing the same jacket as last night, a battered denim thing with a frayed collar, and he was holding a plastic cup of what I assumed was the same terrible coffee.

He stopped when he saw me.

For a moment, neither of us moved. The vending machine hummed. Gerald's torch swept across the far platform. Somewhere in the distance, a train announced its arrival in that bored voice.

"You're here," Bayo said.

"I'm here," I agreed.

"I missed my bus."

"You're chronically late. I'm not surprised."

He grinned, and it was the same easy grin from last night, but there was something underneath it now—something uncertain, almost shy. "I didn't miss it, actually. I just didn't get on it."

"Why not?"

He walked over and sat down on the bench next to me, setting his guitar case on the ground between us. "I was thinking about what you said."

"What did I say?"

"You said nobody was waiting for you." He looked at me, and in the dim light, his eyes were impossibly dark and warm. "I wanted to be somebody waiting for you."

I opened my mouth to say something—I don't know what, something sharp and defensive, something that would protect me from the sheer audacity of a man saying something like that with a straight face—but what came out instead was: "That's the second most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me about a transportation mishap."

"What's the first?"

"You said it last night."

He laughed, and the sound of it filled the empty waiting area like light. "So I've got a two-for-one deal going. I should write that down."

"Don't quit your day job."

"I don't have a day job."

"Then don't quit your night job either."

We sat there for a moment, the silence between us comfortable and charged at the same time. The clock ticked. The vending machine hummed. Something felt different about this night, and I couldn't quite put my finger on what.

"You know," Bayo said, "I had a whole thing planned."

"A whole thing?"

"A speech. I practiced it on the bus over." He gestured vaguely toward the doors. "Something about destiny and timetables and the universe conspiring to bring two chronically unlucky people together at the exact same moment."

"That sounds terrible."

"It was. I'm glad I forgot it."

"Did you forget it?"

He looked at me, and there was something in his expression that made my chest ache. "No," he said. "I just realized it wasn't true."

"What's true, then?"

He was quiet for a long moment. The clock ticked. The vending machine hummed. The world outside the grimy windows continued to turn, indifferent to the two of us sitting in the fluorescent glow of a place where nobody stayed.

"The truth is," he said finally, "I don't know why I came here tonight. I just knew I had to. I knew that if I didn't, I'd spend the rest of my life wondering what would have happened if I had."

"That's a lot of pressure for a bus station."

"It's not the bus station," he said. "It's you."

And there it was again—that feeling, like the ground had shifted slightly beneath me, like I was standing on a platform that was about to move.

I looked at him. He looked at me. The vending machine hummed its sad little hum.

"So," I said, "do you have your guitar?"

He blinked, thrown by the abrupt change in subject. "Yeah. Why?"

"Play for me."

"Right now?"

"Right now. You've got four hours. Show me what you've got."

He laughed, shaking his head, but he was already reaching for his case. "You know, most people ask for my name before they ask for a private concert."

"I already know your name. It's Bayo."

"You don't know my last name."

"I don't need your last name. I need to hear that song again."

He paused, his hand on the zipper. "The unfinished one?"

"The unfinished one."

He looked at me for a long moment, something unreadable passing through his eyes. Then he smiled—a real smile, not the easy grin he'd worn before—and unzipped his guitar case.

"Alright," he said. "But I'm warning you. It's still not finished."

"Good," I said. "I'd hate for you to run out of reasons to come back."

He pulled out the guitar—the same battered acoustic, covered in stickers from places I'd never been—and settled it on his knee. He strummed once, twice, testing the tuning. Then he started to play.

It was the same melody, but different. Slower. Wistful. Like it was remembering something that hadn't happened yet. He still didn't sing, just played, his fingers moving over the strings with that same ease, that same conversation I couldn't quite translate but could almost understand.

When he finished, he looked up at me, and there was something vulnerable in his expression that made me want to reach out and touch his face.

"It's getting better," I said.

"It's still not finished."

"Nothing ever is."

He smiled, slow and real. "You said that last night."

"I meant it last night. I mean it tonight."

We sat there in the quiet, the last notes of the melody still hanging in the air like dust motes in the fluorescent light. The clock ticked toward 00:30. Gerald's torch swept past the windows.

"Bayo," I said.

"Yeah?"

"What time is your bus tomorrow?"

He looked at me, and something shifted in his expression—surprise, maybe, or hope. "I don't know," he said. "I haven't checked."

"Check."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know what time to be here."

He stared at me for a long moment. Then he pulled out his phone, and I watched his thumb move across the screen, and I felt something settle in my chest—something warm and terrifying and entirely unlike me.

"The 4:35," he said. "Same as yours."

"Then I'll be here."

"You will?"

"I will."

He looked at me, and in his eyes I saw something I recognized—that same reckless hope I'd been trying to ignore since the moment I'd walked into this bus station and seen him sitting there with his terrible coffee and his unfinished song.

"Good," he said. "Because I've got a new song to play for you."

"What's it called?"

He grinned, and it was the easy grin, but there was something underneath it now—something real. "I haven't finished writing it yet."

"Then you'll have to come back tomorrow."

"I will," he said. "I'll always come back."

And I believed him.
